Context

APEX STUDENT is an AI scheduling app. I joined when the product existed and the brand did not: no site, no visual language, no personality. The scope was the whole surface — everything someone sees before deciding whether to hand over their calendar.

The problem

Students need structure and resent being told to have it. An app that markets like a productivity coach gets ignored. One that is too playful does not get trusted with a semester. The brand had to sit between those two, and the product had to feel like relief rather than another thing to keep up with.

The thinking

So the AI got a personality rather than an interface. Scheduling is already stressful, and a system that only reports back at you makes it worse.

That is LOLA. She can say things a SaaS brand cannot — she will point out that you blocked four hours of grind time and opened TikTok — and she gives every surface the same narrator, from onboarding through to empty states. The rules about where she does not appear matter just as much. Nobody wants a mascot on a billing screen or an error.

The work

Brand system

Logo, color, type and voice around a single idea: calm structure, warm voice. Then a face to carry it.

Product UI

The app end to end: today view, calendar, the chat with LOLA, the efficiency map, the weekly plan, the menu, the login. The same voice runs from the marketing site straight into the product, so nothing changes register at the door.

Social content

A card format that turns each thing the app claims — clarity, balance, performance — into a slide LOLA delivers herself.

Onboarding

The first run designed as a moment with LOLA rather than a form. Whatever the brand promised outside the app has to survive the loading screen.

Loading Animation

Killed direction

We started as a generic calendar app — the kind nobody opens twice. We killed it. What replaced it is closer to encouragement than to scheduling: something that argues for your day rather than just ruling it into boxes.

Where it stands

APEX shipped. It is on the App Store, which is the point at which a brand stops being a set of files and has to hold up somewhere it cannot be art-directed — an icon two hundred pixels wide, a screenshot carousel, and a first run in front of someone who has never heard of us.

Brand system
Logo, mark, palette, type, key visual and the rules that hold them together — plus LOLA, drawn as a full character set rather than a single mascot pose.
Surfaces
The website, the blog and newsletter, onboarding, in-app illustration and the social content engine.
What we learned
Content with LOLA in it does better than content without her. That is our own read rather than a measured study — but it is the argument for building a character instead of a logo.
Shipped
Live on the App Store. Everything built before launch — the system, the character, the UI, the onboarding — is what the product ships with.
What I'd do next

Now there are installs to measure against, so the next job is retention rather than acquisition: streak moments, a semester recap, and a TikTok account where the character posts and the company stays out of it.
